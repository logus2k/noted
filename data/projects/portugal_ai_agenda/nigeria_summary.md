# Nigeria — National Artificial Intelligence Strategy (Sep 2025)

**Source:** [Nigeria National-Artificial-Intelligence-Strategy-19092025.pdf](../../domains/national_ai_agendas/sources/Nigeria%20National-Artificial-Intelligence-Strategy-19092025.pdf), 58+ pages, published September 2025 by the **Federal Ministry of Communication, Innovation and Digital Economy (FMCIDE)**. Acknowledged contributors include **Lagos Business School**, **Data Science Nigeria**, the **National Information Technology Development Agency (NITDA)**, **Nigeria Communications Commission (NCC)**, **Galaxy Backbone (GBB)**, and other stakeholders.

The NAIS is described as **"a living document to guide Nigeria's adoption of artificial intelligence for socio-economic change"** [p.4]. Five-year implementation horizon: **2025–2029**.

## Strategic framing

> "To be a global leader in AI, leveraging ethical and inclusive innovation, local talent, and strategic collaboration to drive sustainable development." [Vision, p.28]

The NAIS is anchored to (a) **Nigeria's national development goals**, (b) the **FMCIDE strategic plan** ("Accelerating our Collective Prosperity through Technical Efficiency"), and (c) the **NITDA Strategic Roadmap and Action Plan 2** [p.4]. It builds on prior instruments including the **National Digital Economy Policy and Strategy (NDEPS)** and the **Nigeria Data Protection Act, 2023 (NDPA)**.

The headline positioning: Nigeria as **Africa's most populous country**, **median age ~18**, with **~70% of Nigerians under 30**, claiming a uniquely large human-capital base for AI [p.35]. **74% of the worldwide increase in youth aged 18–23 between 2015 and 2035 will occur in Nigeria and nine other countries** (World Bank) [p.35].

## Three strategic objectives [p.30]

1. **Economic Growth and Competitiveness** — boost productivity, create new industries and jobs, attract foreign investment
2. **Social Development and Inclusion** — improve access to healthcare/education/financial inclusion, address poverty, inequality, climate change, empower citizens
3. **Technological Advancement and Leadership** — develop indigenous AI expertise, establish ethical and regulatory frameworks, become regional and global leader

## Nine guiding principles [p.28-29]

| Principle | Coverage |
|---|---|
| Responsible and Ethical Conduct | Lifecycle ethics, transparency, fairness, accountability, human rights |
| Inclusivity and Shared Prosperity | No-one-left-behind, diversity, marginalised populations |
| Innovation and Adaptation | Experimentation, risk-taking, continuous improvement |
| Sustainability | Environmental conservation, long-term goals |
| Collaboration | Multi-stakeholder, international |
| Global Leadership | International standard-setting |
| Transparency and Accountability | Open, clear, auditable AI systems |
| Human-Centric | Augment, not replace, human agency |
| Risk Management and Resilience | Robust mitigation, adaptive systems |

A tenth thread on **Data Protection** is treated explicitly alongside the principles, anchored to NDPA 2023 [p.29].

## Five strategic pillars (34 strategies total) [p.31]

| Pillar | Focus |
|---|---|
| **1. Building Foundational AI Infrastructure** | HPC resources, AI-specific hardware/software, clean-energy AI clusters, "Pioneer status for AI" tax incentives |
| **2. Building and Sustaining a World-class AI Ecosystem** | Partnerships, international collaboration, innovation culture, talent transfer, commercialisation, AI Centres of Excellence, national AI conference |
| **3. Accelerating AI Adoption and Sector Transformation** | Locally-led innovation, data-driven decision-making, AI-ready workforce, widespread sectoral integration, green AI |
| **4. Ensuring Responsible and Ethical AI Development** | AI Ethics Expert Group, national ethical principles (including **decoloniality**), assessment framework, human-centred transition |
| **5. Developing a Robust AI Governance Framework** | National AI Principles, AI Governance Regulatory Body, National AI Policy Framework, AI Risk Management Framework, **AIGN** (Artificial Intelligence Governance Network) alignment |

### Pillar 1 — Foundational Infrastructure (Objective 1.1)

- **1.1.1 Deploy High-Performance Computing (HPC) Resources** — national HPC centres for researchers, startups, businesses
- **1.1.2 AI-specific hardware and software development** — explicit preference for **domestically produced** solutions
- **1.1.3 Clean and sustainable energy for AI clusters** — **keep grid dependence below 50% per cluster**
- **1.1.4 "Pioneer status for AI"** — tax breaks and incentives for AI edge computing stations and HPC data centres

### Pillar 2 — Ecosystem (Objectives 2.1–2.3)

- **2.1.1 Sustainable Applied AI Partnership Platform (SAAPP) / Collaborative Innovation Platform** — academia + industry + government + civil society co-creation
- **2.2.1 AI Synergy Alliance / National AI Collaboration Office** — formal alliance with top-tier global AI research institutions and companies
- **2.2.2 Nigerian AI Talent Transfer Program** — attract and retain global AI talent into Nigeria
- **2.2.3 Nigerian AI Partnership for Commercialisation Excellence (NAPCE)** — research-to-market bridge
- **2.3.1 AI Centres of Excellence (CoEs)** — urban and rural mainstreaming
- **2.3.2 Annual National AI Conference** with international participation

### Pillar 3 — Adoption (Objectives 3.1–3.5)

- **3.1.1 "Roadmapping"** — strategic lighthouse projects across agriculture, healthcare, education, finance, government
- **3.1.2 Sector-specific AI adoption roadmaps**
- **3.2.1 Adopt globally recognised AI data quality standards**
- **3.2.2 Open Data Initiative (ODI)** — public-private data collaboration
- **3.3.1 AI training and education systems** — curriculum reform, teacher training, reskilling
- **3.3.2 National AI R&D Fund (AI-R&D Fund)**
- **3.3.3 National Research and Education Network (NREN)** — high-speed research network
- **3.4.1 AI Policy Advocacy** — enabling regulatory environment
- **3.4.2 Tripartite AI Transfer Partnership Program** — research, business, government
- **3.4.3 Annual AI Adoption Summits and Hackathons**
- **3.5.1 Green AI Challenge and Grant Programme** — climate, waste, sustainable resource use, smart cities
- **3.5.2 Green AI adoption across sectors**

### Pillar 4 — Ethics (Objectives 4.1–4.2)

- **4.1.1 High-Level AI Ethics Expert Group (AIEEG) / National AI Ethics Commission** — diverse advisory group
- **4.1.2 National AI Ethical Principles** — explicitly including **decoloniality**, humanity, technical robustness, inclusion, non-discrimination, accountability, solidarity, transparency, responsible data governance
- **4.1.3 AI ethics assessment framework** — lifecycle-wide evaluation
- **4.2.1 Horizon scan / foresight study** — map AI's impact on inequalities, jobs, vulnerable groups
- **4.2.2 Legislative reforms** — workers' rights, retraining, unemployment benefits, digital-literacy access

### Pillar 5 — Governance (Objective 5.1)

- **5.1.1 National AI Principles**
- **5.1.2 AI Governance Regulatory Body** (NITDA-promoted) — independent oversight
- **5.1.3 National AI Policy Framework**
- **5.1.4 National AI Risk Management Framework**

The pillar references the **AIGN (Artificial Intelligence Governance Network) Framework** [p.46] as the modular, operational, certifiable governance system Nigeria is aligning to, alongside the **EU AI Act** as an emerging international benchmark.

## Risk register [p.54]

Six named risks with explicit likelihood / impact / rating:

| Risk | Likelihood | Impact | Rating | Key mitigations |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Ethical Concerns** | High | High | **Extreme** | AI ethics principles, regulatory frameworks, public awareness campaigns |
| **Funding Shortfall** | Medium | High | High | Public-private funding, cost-effectiveness prioritisation, innovative financing (PPPs) |
| **Lack of Skilled Workforce** | Medium | High | High | AI Talent Transfer Programme, university partnerships, international collaboration |
| **Data Security Breaches** | Medium | High | High | Encryption, access controls, cybersecurity investment |
| **Rapid Technological Change** | High | Medium | High | Continuous monitoring, innovation culture, ongoing R&D |
| **Slow Private-Sector Adoption** | Medium | Medium | Medium | Tax incentives, case-study showcasing, industry-association partnerships |

The explicit naming of **Ethical Concerns as Extreme** is distinctive: most comparator strategies treat ethics as a guiding principle but not as the single highest-rated implementation risk.

## Institutional architecture and existing instruments

- **FMCIDE** — line ministry, strategy owner
- **NITDA** — promoter of the AI Governance Regulatory Body; existing technology development agency
- **NCC** — Nigeria Communications Commission, contributor
- **Galaxy Backbone (GBB)** — public ICT backbone
- **NERDC** — Nigerian Educational Research and Development Council, AI/robotics primary-curriculum integration (2023 directive)
- **NITDA-funded innovation hubs** — **NitHub (University of Lagos)**, **Lion Science Park (University of Nigeria)**, **Tech-PIC (Federal University of Technology, Akure)**
- **3MTT (Three Million Technical Talent)** — train 3 million Nigerians in tech skills over four years
- **NDEPS** — National Digital Economy Policy and Strategy (prior overarching frame)
- **NDPA 2023** — Nigeria Data Protection Act (data-governance anchor)
- **House of Representatives Committee on Information and Cybercrime** — legislative AI engagement

## Regulation posture

Nigeria has **no AI-specific Act yet**. The NAIS commits to **developing a National AI Policy Framework** under Pillar 5. The regulatory backbone is built on:

- **NDPA 2023** for data protection
- **Cybercrime regulations**
- **NOTAP** (National Office for Technology Acquisition and Promotion)
- **NITDA Act**
- **NUC** (National Universities Commission) for AI-curriculum oversight

The strategy explicitly references the **EU AI Act** and the **AIGN Framework** as international benchmarks for governance design.

## Continental and international positioning

The NAIS positions Nigeria as **Africa's AI flagship**, building on its self-described status as one of Africa's **"Big Four" tech countries**. Strategy 2.2 (Champion International Collaboration and Leadership) makes this explicit. The brain-drain finding is framed candidly: Nigeria is the **third-largest source of UK Global Tech Talent Visa applicants worldwide**, with **11.3% of global applicants** (Tech Nation 2021).

## What Nigeria does that Portugal's ANIA does NOT

1. **Explicit youth-demographic anchoring** — median age 18, 70% under 30, 74% of global youth 18–23 growth 2015–2035 from Nigeria + 9 countries. The strategy treats demographics as the central national asset. Portugal's ANIA correctly notes its smaller population but does not anchor strategy to a demographic positioning.

2. **Concrete workforce target** — ≥70% of 16–35-year-olds (3MTT trains 3 million in 4 years), with **50% women** explicit. Portugal's Talent pillar (III) sets per-initiative targets but no whole-cohort headline equivalent.

3. **Explicit "decoloniality" in the ethical principles list** — Nigeria names decoloniality among its national AI ethical principles (P4.1.2). Portugal's IV pillar (Responsibility) and EU AI Act anchoring do not name decoloniality as a distinct principle.

4. **Domestic AI hardware/software preference (P1.1.2)** — Nigeria explicitly states preference for domestically produced AI hardware (e.g., specialised chips) and software, to reduce foreign-tech dependence. Portugal's ANIA does not name a similar industrial-sovereignty commitment.

5. **Clean-energy AI clusters with grid-dependence cap (P1.1.3)** — Nigeria caps national-grid dependence of each AI cluster at <50%, with the balance from clean energy. Portugal has no equivalent explicit grid-mix cap for AI compute.

6. **"Pioneer status for AI" tax-incentive framework (P1.1.4)** — leverages an existing Nigerian incentive instrument. Portugal references EU funding instruments but does not name a Portuguese-specific tax-incentive class for AI.

7. **Explicit six-risk register with rating** — Funding Shortfall, Skills, Ethics (Extreme), Data Security, Slow Adoption, Rapid Tech Change. Portugal's risk treatment is implicit within initiative cards; no equivalent named risk matrix.

8. **AIGN Framework alignment** as the operational governance system. Portugal aligns to EU AI Act + GDPR; the AIGN choice is distinctive.

9. **National Research and Education Network (NREN) as a named initiative (P3.3.3)** — Portugal's research-infrastructure thread is bundled in the Development pillar without a named NREN equivalent.

10. **Tripartite AI Transfer Partnership Program (P3.4.2)** — research–business–government as a single named instrument. Portugal has the sectoral AI centres (II.5) but no equivalent tripartite-knowledge-transfer named instrument.

11. **Annual National AI Conference with international participation (P2.3.3)** — Nigeria names this as a strategy. Portugal does not commit to an equivalent annual national AI event.

12. **Green AI Challenge and Grant Programme (P3.5.1)** as a named instrument linking AI to climate, waste, smart cities. Portugal touches AI-sustainability via cross-cutting language but no named challenge programme.

## What Portugal's ANIA does that Nigeria's NAIS does NOT

1. **EU AI Act + GDPR as binding regulatory anchors** — Nigeria references the EU AI Act as benchmark only; Portugal commits to operationalising it.

2. **Per-initiative cards** with named responsible entities, start semesters (2S 2025 → 1S 2027), and indicative budgets. Nigeria has 34 strategies but they are written as outcome + strategy paragraphs without per-strategy owner, semester, or budget.

3. **Sovereign-language LLM commitment (AMALIA)** — Nigeria's infrastructure pillar names HPC and hardware but no specific national-language model commitment for Nigerian-language coverage (Hausa, Yoruba, Igbo).

4. **Quantified macroeconomic case** (€18–22B/yr GDP add, +2.7pp productivity). Nigeria cites jobs/skills demographics (28M digital-skills jobs, $130B market) but no headline national GDP uplift attributed to AI specifically.

5. **Named strategic technology bets** (Cybersecurity, LLMs, AI+Robotics for Industry, Blue Economy, Responsible AI). Nigeria names broad sectors (agriculture, healthcare, education, finance, government) but no equivalent enumeration of strategic technology bets.

6. **AI literacy citizen campaign (III.8)** — Nigeria's training programmes target workforce upskilling but do not name a broad citizen-facing literacy campaign equivalent.

7. **Regulatory sandbox as a forward initiative (IV.5)** — Nigeria's regulatory roadmap covers oversight body and frameworks but not a named sandbox instrument.

8. **EU-anchored funding context** — Portugal can draw on PRR / Horizon Europe / EuroHPC funding. Nigeria's funding model relies on national budget, PPPs, and the explicit risk flag of funding shortfall.

## Critique-lens conclusion

Nigeria's NAIS is the **most demographically-anchored** strategy in the comparator set: it builds the entire national value proposition on the **youth bulge** and treats the 3MTT (Three Million Technical Talent) programme as the headline talent instrument. Its strongest distinctive features are (a) the **explicit decoloniality principle** in the ethics list, (b) the **clean-energy AI cluster cap (<50% grid dependence)**, (c) the **explicit risk matrix with Ethics rated Extreme**, and (d) the **domestic AI hardware/software preference** as an industrial-sovereignty stance.

Its weakest features for cross-comparison are (a) the **absence of per-strategy ownership and budgets**, (b) the **lack of a national-language LLM commitment** in a country with hundreds of languages, and (c) the **funding shortfall risk** which the document itself flags as High.

### Top moves Portugal could import from Nigeria

1. **Youth-demographic narrative**, adapted for Portugal's inverse situation: where Nigeria builds strategy around its young population, Portugal could explicitly frame the AI agenda around **demographic ageing as the strategic problem AI must address** (elderly care, healthcare productivity, labour-shortage compensation, intergenerational knowledge capture). This is currently implicit in ANIA II.4 (Health) but not framed as a national positioning.

2. **Explicit ethical principle of decoloniality / cultural specificity** in the Responsibility pillar. Portugal does not name a comparable principle; an equivalent for Portugal might be **"cultural and linguistic specificity"** or **"non-extractive AI"** applied to Portuguese language and Lusophone communities. The Portuguese-language LLM (AMALIA) gives the substrate; a named principle would give the frame.

3. **Clean-energy AI cluster cap** (Nigeria's <50% grid-dependence rule). Portugal's National Data Centre Plan (I.4) does not include an equivalent renewables-mix cap for AI compute. Adding one would tie the AI agenda to Portugal's renewable-energy advantage (~70% renewable electricity already).

4. **Explicit risk matrix** with named risks, likelihood, impact, rating, and mitigation. Portugal's initiative cards have implicit risk discussion; a standalone risk register would mirror Nigeria's Pillar 6 (risk treatment) and the UK's approach. Portugal-specific risks worth naming: funding cliff after PRR, EU AI Act compliance delays, brain-drain to other EU states, energy-intensity backlash, public-trust collapse in algorithmic public-sector decisions.

5. **National Research and Education Network (NREN) as a named instrument**. Portugal has FCCN (the academic network within FCT) but does not name it as a strategic AI-data backbone the way Nigeria names NREN.

6. **Tripartite AI Transfer Partnership Program** (research–business–government). Portugal has sectoral centres (II.5) and the IA Responsável centre (IV.3) but no equivalent named tripartite knowledge-transfer instrument.

7. **Domestic AI hardware/software preference** at a Portugal-realistic scale. Portugal cannot replicate Nigerian-style chip-design ambition, but it could name a **"Portuguese AI software stack preference"** in public procurement (open-source + EU-sovereign hosting + Portuguese-language LLMs) under ANIA II.13 (Procurement Code Guide).

8. **Annual National AI Conference**. Portugal hosts academic AI conferences but does not name an annual national flagship event in the ANIA. A named **"Cimeira Nacional de IA"** under IV.3 would parallel Nigeria's P2.3.3.

### What Portugal should NOT copy

- **The youth-bulge narrative directly** — Portugal's demographics are the opposite; using the same framing would land badly. The *structural move* (anchor strategy to a demographic reality) is importable; the specific positioning is not.
- **The absence of per-initiative budgets** — Portugal's specificity is the better template; Nigeria's outcome+strategy paragraphs are looser.
- **The "no national-language LLM" silence** — Portugal is correct to commit to AMALIA; replicating Nigeria's silence on local-language LLMs would be a regression.
- **The funding-shortfall risk acceptance** — Nigeria flags this candidly because the strategy depends on uncertain budget. Portugal's EU-funding context is more secure and should not be diluted by importing Nigeria's funding-fragility framing.

### Strategic complementarity with other comparators

Nigeria's strongest distinctive moves combine well with UK operational rigour and Spain's per-initiative budget transparency. An ideal Portuguese ANIA evolution drawing from Nigeria specifically would adopt:

- **Nigerian demographic anchoring pattern** (applied inversely, to ageing)
- **Nigerian explicit risk matrix** (Pillar 5/6 style)
- **Nigerian clean-energy AI cluster cap**
- **Nigerian decoloniality / cultural-specificity ethical principle**
- **Nigerian Tripartite AI Transfer Partnership** model
